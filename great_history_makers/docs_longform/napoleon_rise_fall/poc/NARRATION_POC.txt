December 1805. One year after crowning himself Emperor of the French, Napoleon Bonaparte stands on a frozen hillside in Moravia, watching the armies of two emperors gather in the fog. He is thirty-six years old.
Three months earlier, his Grande Armée was camped on the Channel coast. In a few weeks it marched to the Rhine, and seven corps swung across southern Germany, cutting off an Austrian army at Ulm. On the twentieth of October, General Mack surrendered. In November, the French entered Vienna.
At dawn on the second of December, the Russians and Austrians attack the French right. Then, as the sun burns through the mist, Marshal Soult's divisions storm the Pratzen Heights at the centre of the allied line, and split their army in two. By nightfall, the coalition is broken.
Seven years later, the same army would be dying in the snow, on the long road back from Moscow.
This is the story of how Napoleon rose so far, and how he fell.
