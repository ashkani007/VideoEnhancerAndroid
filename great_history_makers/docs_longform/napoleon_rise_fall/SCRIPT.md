## Cold open: Waterloo, 18 June 1815 (about 1:30)

> *Picture: rain-soaked fields at dawn, mud, a farmhouse on fire; the Emperor on a low rise with a telescope; French columns forming. Map beat: the three armies converge on Waterloo.*

On the morning of the eighteenth of June, 1815, the ground south of Brussels was still soaked from a night of rain.
Two armies faced each other across a shallow valley. On one ridge stood the Duke of Wellington, with an army of British, Dutch, Belgian and German troops. On the other stood Napoleon Bonaparte, Emperor of the French, back from exile for one last campaign.

He waited for the ground to dry before opening fire. The delay mattered: a Prussian army under Marshal Blücher, beaten by Napoleon only two days earlier, was marching toward the battlefield from the east. Wellington had chosen to stand here because Blücher had promised to come.

All afternoon, French infantry and cavalry threw themselves at Wellington's line: at the farm of Hougoumont, at La Haye Sainte, and in wave after wave of cavalry against squares of redcoats that would not break.
In the evening, with Prussian corps pressing into his right flank, Napoleon sent forward his last reserve, the Imperial Guard. The Guard was driven back down the slope. The French army dissolved into retreat.

It was the end of twenty years of war, and of one of the most extraordinary careers in history.
To understand how it ended in a muddy Belgian valley, we have to go back to an island in the Mediterranean.

## 1. Corsica and the Revolution (about 2:15)

> *Picture: Ajaccio harbour, 1770s; a young boy among Corsican hills; the military school at Brienne; Paris in revolution. Map beat: Corsica, newly French, and the route to mainland schools.*

Napoleone di Buonaparte was born in Ajaccio, Corsica, on the fifteenth of August, 1769, the year after the island passed from Genoa to France.
His family belonged to the minor Corsican nobility. That status won him a royal scholarship, and at nine he was sent to the mainland: first to the military school at Brienne, then to the École Militaire in Paris.
He was an outsider with a heavy accent, and a voracious reader of history and mathematics. As a young man he dreamed of Corsican independence, not of France. In 1785, at sixteen, he was commissioned as a second lieutenant of artillery, the most technical and least aristocratic branch of the army.

Four years later the Revolution swept away the old monarchy. It also swept away the old officer class, and opened careers to men of talent.
Bonaparte's chance came in 1793 at the port of Toulon, which royalists had handed to a British fleet. The young artillery captain organised the batteries that made the harbour untenable for the British. After Toulon fell, he was promoted brigadier general at twenty-four.
The fall of Robespierre in 1794 brought him a brief arrest and months without a command. Then, in October 1795, in Paris, he helped crush a royalist uprising against the government with artillery fire in the streets. The Republic now owed him a debt, and gave him the command of the army of the interior.
In March 1796 he married Joséphine de Beauharnais. Two days later he left to take command of the Army of Italy.

## 2. Italy, Egypt and the seizure of power (about 2:15)

> *Picture: a ragged army in the Alpine foothills; the bridge at Lodi; the dykes of Arcole; the pyramids; Saint-Cloud, November 1799. Map beat: the Italian campaign of 1796–97, with army movements against Piedmont and Austria.*

The Army of Italy was unpaid, underfed and badly equipped. Within weeks its new commander had knocked Piedmont out of the war. He then drove the Austrians back across northern Italy at Lodi, Arcole and Rivoli.
He moved faster than his enemies expected, concentrated his forces where they were weakest, and wrote his own victory bulletins for the newspapers in Paris. At Arcole he was nearly killed leading an attack across a causeway through the marshes; a painting of the scene, with a young general carrying a flag, would become one of the first images of his legend.
By 1797 he was dictating peace terms to Austria, without waiting for instructions from Paris. He was twenty-eight.

The next year he led an expedition to Egypt, a bold plan to threaten Britain's route to India. With the soldiers came more than a hundred and fifty scholars and scientists, and it was during the expedition that French soldiers found the Rosetta Stone. He won battles on land, including the Battle of the Pyramids. But at Aboukir Bay, Admiral Nelson destroyed the French fleet, and the army was stranded. A campaign into Syria failed before the walls of Acre, and plague spread through the ranks.
In 1799 Bonaparte left the army in Egypt, slipped back to France, and found the government divided and unpopular. In November he joined a coup that overthrew the Directory and became First Consul: in practice, the ruler of France. Seven months later he crossed the Alps and defeated the Austrians at Marengo, and a fragile peace followed with Austria and then with Britain.

## 3. Emperor, and the sun of Austerlitz (about 2:30)

> *Picture: the Consulate at work; the coronation in Notre-Dame; the Boulogne camp; mist over the Pratzen Heights. Map beat (the POC map): the Ulm–Austerlitz campaign, with the corps moving from the Rhine to the Danube, the encirclement at Ulm, then Vienna and Austerlitz.*

As First Consul, Bonaparte rebuilt the French state.
- He made peace with the Catholic Church in the Concordat.
- He founded the Bank of France and the Legion of Honour.
- He sent prefects to run every department from Paris.
- In 1804 he promulgated the Civil Code, a single body of law for all of France that would outlive him.

That same year he took the final step. On the second of December, 1804, in Notre-Dame, in the presence of the Pope, he crowned himself Emperor of the French.

Britain had been at war with France again since 1803, and in 1805 Austria and Russia joined a new coalition.
Napoleon turned the army he had gathered on the Channel coast into the Grande Armée. Seven corps marched to the Rhine and swung across southern Germany so fast that an Austrian army under General Mack was cut off and surrounded at Ulm. On the twentieth of October, Mack surrendered. In November the French entered Vienna.
A day after Ulm fell, Nelson destroyed the combined French and Spanish fleets at Trafalgar. Britain would rule the seas.

On land, the decisive moment came on the second of December 1805, a year to the day after the coronation, near the Moravian village of Austerlitz.
Napoleon deliberately weakened his right to tempt the Russian and Austrian armies into attacking it. When they did, Marshal Soult's troops climbed out of the morning mist and seized the Pratzen Heights at the centre of the allied line, splitting their army in two.
By nightfall the coalition was broken. Austria made peace within weeks, and the Russian army withdrew to the east. Austerlitz became the model of a Napoleonic victory, studied by soldiers for the next hundred years.

## 4. Master of Europe (about 2:00)

> *Picture: Jena; the meeting on the raft at Tilsit; Spanish guerrillas; Wagram. Map beat: the Empire and its satellite kingdoms spreading to 1811; the Continental System.*

In 1806 Prussia went to war and was crushed in a single day at Jena and Auerstedt. In 1807, after the bloody stalemate of Eylau and the victory of Friedland, Napoleon met Tsar Alexander on a raft in the river Niemen and made peace at Tilsit.
He redrew the map of Europe:
- He abolished the Holy Roman Empire.
- He created the Confederation of the Rhine and the Duchy of Warsaw.
- He placed his brothers on the thrones of Holland, Naples, Westphalia and Spain.

To strangle Britain's economy, he ordered a European blockade of British trade, the Continental System.
But the system was hard to enforce. In Spain and Portugal, it drew him into a war of occupation, insurgency and British intervention that would cost hundreds of thousands of lives.
In 1809 Austria rose again. Napoleon suffered his first major check at Aspern-Essling, then won at Wagram after two days of the largest battle Europe had yet seen. In 1810 he divorced Joséphine, who had given him no heir, and married the Austrian archduchess Marie-Louise. In 1811 their son was born and given the title King of Rome.
On paper, the Empire was at its height: France itself stretched from Hamburg to Rome, surrounded by allied and dependent states. But it rested on conscription, taxation and the loyalty of reluctant allies.

## 5. The invasion of Russia (about 2:15)

> *Picture: the crossing of the Niemen; dust, heat and abandoned carts; Borodino; Moscow in flames. Map beat: the invasion route from the Niemen to Moscow, with the shrinking army strength shown as a band.*

Tsar Alexander withdrew from the Continental System, and both sides prepared for war.
On the twenty-fourth of June, 1812, the largest army Europe had ever seen began to cross the river Niemen into Russia. Historians' estimates for the whole invasion force range from about 450,000 to over 600,000 men, and roughly half of them came from outside France: from Poland, Germany, Italy and the Empire's other states.

The Russians retreated, refusing a decisive battle and burning supplies as they went. Summer heat, disease and desertion thinned the French ranks long before any snow fell. Horses died in their thousands. At Smolensk, in August, the city burned and the Russians slipped away again.
On the seventh of September, at Borodino, seventy miles west of Moscow, the two armies finally met in one of the bloodiest single days of the century. Tens of thousands fell on both sides. Napoleon refused to commit his Imperial Guard, and the Russians withdrew in order.
A week later Napoleon entered Moscow. Within hours, fires broke out across the city, and most of Moscow burned.
He waited more than a month for the Tsar to negotiate. No answer came.

## 6. Retreat and collapse (about 2:00)

> *Picture: the long column on the Smolensk road; snow; the bridges over the Berezina; frozen survivors at the Niemen. Map beat: the retreat route, the Berezina crossing, then the 1813 coalition converging on Leipzig.*

On the nineteenth of October, 1812, the Grande Armée left Moscow.
It was forced back along the same devastated road it had come by. Hunger, disease, exhaustion and Cossack raids destroyed it piece by piece. Marshal Ney commanded the rearguard, fighting off attacks day after day. The severe frosts of November and December completed the work.
At the river Berezina, in late November, engineers working in icy water built the bridges that let part of the army escape. In December, only a fraction of the force that had entered Russia struggled back across the Niemen.
Napoleon had already left for Paris to raise a new army.

He did raise one, and won more victories in 1813. But Prussia, then Austria, joined Russia, Britain and Sweden.
In October 1813, at Leipzig, the "Battle of the Nations", more than half a million soldiers fought for three days, and the coalition defeated him. In 1814 allied armies entered France. With a small army of veterans and young conscripts, Napoleon won a string of brilliant victories in February, but he could not be everywhere. Paris fell at the end of March, and his marshals refused to fight on. On the sixth of April, Napoleon abdicated. He was sent into exile on the island of Elba, off the Italian coast, and allowed to keep the title of Emperor of a tiny realm.

## 7. The Hundred Days and Waterloo (about 1:45)

> *Picture: a small flotilla off Golfe-Juan; soldiers sent to arrest him lowering their muskets; the Tuileries at night; Ligny; Waterloo at evening. Map beat: the return route from Golfe-Juan via Grenoble and Lyon to Paris, then the Belgian campaign of June 1815.*

He stayed less than a year.
On the first of March, 1815, Napoleon landed at Golfe-Juan with about a thousand men and marched on Paris through the mountains. Near Grenoble, troops sent to stop him went over to his side. On the twentieth of March he was back in the Tuileries. King Louis the Eighteenth had fled.

The allies declared him an outlaw and mobilised again. Napoleon struck first, into Belgium, hoping to defeat the British and Prussian armies separately.
On the sixteenth of June he beat the Prussians at Ligny, but did not destroy them. Two days later came Waterloo. The Prussians arrived, as Blücher had promised Wellington, and the battle was lost.
The cost of that single day was immense: tens of thousands of men killed or wounded in an area of a few square kilometres.
On the twenty-second of June, Napoleon abdicated for the second time. He considered escaping to America, but British ships blockaded the coast. Three weeks later he surrendered to the captain of a British warship.

## 8. Saint Helena and the legend (about 2:15)

> *Picture: HMS Northumberland at sea; the cliffs of Saint Helena; Longwood House; the return of the ashes to Paris in 1840; Les Invalides. Map beat: the voyage from Europe to the South Atlantic.*

This time the British sent him to Saint Helena, a volcanic island in the South Atlantic, about 1,900 kilometres from the coast of Africa.
At Longwood House he dictated his memories to a small circle of companions, shaping the story of his own life: the defender of the Revolution, the lawgiver, the soldier betrayed by fortune. Their published accounts would build his legend.
His relations with the British governor, Sir Hudson Lowe, were bitter, and his health declined. He died there on the fifth of May, 1821, at the age of fifty-one. His doctors' autopsy pointed to stomach cancer, the cause most historians accept; later theories of arsenic poisoning remain disputed. In 1840 his remains were brought back to Paris and laid to rest under the dome of Les Invalides.

What did he leave behind?
- **What lasted:** the Civil Code, which shaped legal systems across Europe and beyond, and the centralised French state with its prefects, lycées and Bank of France. The map of Germany and Italy never returned to what it had been.
- **What it cost:** wars that killed millions of soldiers and civilians. In 1802 he also restored slavery in the French Caribbean colonies, which the Revolution had abolished.

He rose through the Revolution and ended it. He spread its laws on bayonets. He built an empire that lasted barely a decade.
More than two centuries later, he remains one of the most admired, and most argued-about, figures in history.
