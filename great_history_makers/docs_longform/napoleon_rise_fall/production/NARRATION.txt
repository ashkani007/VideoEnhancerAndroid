[CH1 WATERLOO]
On the morning of the eighteenth of June, 1815, the fields south of Brussels were still soaked from a night of heavy rain.
Two armies faced each other across a shallow valley, roughly seventy thousand men on each side. On one ridge stood the Duke of Wellington, with an army of British, Dutch, Belgian and German troops. On the other stood Napoleon Bonaparte, Emperor of the French, back from exile for one last campaign.
He waited for the ground to dry before opening fire. The delay would matter. A Prussian army under Marshal Blücher, beaten by Napoleon only two days earlier, was marching toward the battlefield from the east. Wellington had chosen to fight here because Blücher had promised to come.
All afternoon, the French attacked. At the farm of Hougoumont, the fighting never stopped. At La Haye Sainte, the defenders held until their ammunition ran out. Wave after wave of French cavalry swept up the slope and broke around squares of redcoats that would not give way.
By early evening, Prussian troops were pushing into the village of Plancenoit, on the French right flank. Napoleon had one reserve left: the Imperial Guard, the veterans who had never been driven from a battlefield.
He ordered them forward.
What happened next would decide the fate of Europe. To understand how this man came to be standing on a muddy Belgian ridge, an emperor, an exile, and an emperor again, we have to go back almost half a century, to an island in the Mediterranean.

[CH2 CORSICA AND THE REVOLUTION]
Napoleone di Buonaparte was born in Ajaccio, on the island of Corsica, on the fifteenth of August, 1769. Only a year earlier, the Republic of Genoa had handed the island to France, and French troops had just crushed the Corsican fight for independence.
His father, Carlo, was a lawyer from the minor nobility who made his peace with the new rulers. That status won his son a royal scholarship, and at the age of nine Napoleone was sent to the mainland: first to the military school at Brienne, then to the École Militaire in Paris.
He was an outsider, mocked for his accent and his poverty, and a voracious reader of history and mathematics. As a young man he dreamed of Corsican freedom, not of French glory. In 1785, at sixteen, he was commissioned as a second lieutenant of artillery, the most technical branch of the army, and one of the few open to a poor nobleman with talent.
Four years later, the French Revolution swept away the old monarchy. It also swept away much of the old officer corps, and opened careers to men of ability.
Bonaparte's chance came in 1793, at the port of Toulon, which royalists had handed over to a British fleet. The young artillery captain placed the guns that made the harbour impossible to hold. The British sailed away, and at twenty-four he was promoted to brigadier general.
The fall of Robespierre brought him a brief arrest and months without a command. Then, in October 1795, he helped the government crush a royalist uprising in the streets of Paris with artillery fire. The Republic now owed him a debt.
In March 1796 he married Joséphine de Beauharnais, a widow with powerful friends. Two days later he left Paris to take command of the Army of Italy.

[CH3 ITALY, EGYPT AND POWER]
The Army of Italy was unpaid, underfed and badly equipped. Its new commander was twenty-six years old, and almost unknown.
Within weeks, he had split the armies of Piedmont and Austria, and forced Piedmont out of the war. Then he drove the Austrians across northern Italy, at Lodi, at Arcole and at Rivoli, and besieged their great fortress of Mantua until it surrendered.
He moved faster than his enemies thought possible, concentrated his forces where they were weakest, and wrote his own victory bulletins for the newspapers in Paris. At Arcole he was nearly killed leading an attack along a causeway through the marshes. A painting of that moment, a young general carrying a flag into the smoke, became one of the first images of his legend.
By 1797 he was dictating peace terms to Austria, without waiting for instructions from Paris.
The next year he sailed for Egypt, a bold plan to threaten Britain's route to India. With his soldiers came more than a hundred and fifty scholars and scientists, and during the expedition French soldiers uncovered the Rosetta Stone. On land he won battles, including the Battle of the Pyramids. But at Aboukir Bay, Admiral Nelson destroyed the French fleet, and the army was trapped. A campaign into Syria failed before the walls of Acre, and plague spread through the ranks.
In August 1799, Bonaparte left his army in Egypt and slipped back to France. He found the government divided and unpopular. In November he joined a coup that overthrew it, and became First Consul: in practice, the ruler of France.
Seven months later, he led an army across the Great Saint Bernard Pass and defeated the Austrians at Marengo. Peace with Austria, and then with Britain, followed. The general from Corsica was thirty years old, and France was his.

[CH4 CORONATION, ULM AND AUSTERLITZ]
As First Consul, Bonaparte rebuilt the French state. He made peace with the Catholic Church. He founded the Bank of France and the Legion of Honour. He sent prefects to govern every department from Paris. And in 1804 he gave France the Civil Code, a single body of law for the whole country, which would outlive him by centuries.
That same year he took the final step. On the second of December, 1804, in the cathedral of Notre-Dame, in the presence of the Pope, he placed the crown on his own head, and became Emperor of the French.
Britain had been at war with France again since 1803. In 1805, Austria and Russia joined a new coalition against him.
Napoleon turned the army he had gathered on the Channel coast around, and marched it to the Rhine. Seven army corps swung across southern Germany so quickly that an Austrian army under General Mack was cut off and surrounded at Ulm. On the twentieth of October, Mack surrendered. In November, the French entered Vienna.
At sea, the news was very different. A day after Ulm, Nelson destroyed the French and Spanish fleets at Trafalgar. Britain would rule the oceans for the rest of the war.
On land, the decisive moment came near the Moravian village of Austerlitz, on the second of December 1805, a year to the day after the coronation.
Outnumbered, Napoleon deliberately weakened his right wing, to tempt the Russian and Austrian armies into attacking it. They took the bait. As the sun burned through the morning mist, Marshal Soult's divisions climbed the Pratzen Heights at the centre of the allied line, and split their army in two.
By nightfall the coalition was broken. Austria made peace within weeks. Austerlitz became the model of a Napoleonic victory, studied by soldiers for the next hundred years.

[CH5 EUROPE AND RUSSIA]
In 1806, Prussia went to war, and its army was destroyed in a single day at Jena and Auerstedt. In 1807, after the frozen slaughter of Eylau and the victory of Friedland, Napoleon met Tsar Alexander on a raft in the middle of the river Niemen, and made peace at Tilsit.
He redrew the map of Europe. He abolished the thousand-year-old Holy Roman Empire, created the Confederation of the Rhine and the Duchy of Warsaw, and placed his brothers on the thrones of Holland, Naples, Westphalia and Spain.
To strangle Britain's economy, he ordered a blockade of British trade across the continent: the Continental System. But it was almost impossible to enforce. In Spain and Portugal it drew him into a long war of occupation, insurgency and British intervention, a war that would cost hundreds of thousands of lives.
In 1809 Austria rose again, and was defeated again, at Wagram. The next year Napoleon divorced Joséphine, who had given him no heir, and married the Austrian archduchess Marie-Louise. In 1811 their son was born, and given the title King of Rome.
On the map, the Empire was at its height. France itself stretched from Hamburg to Rome, surrounded by allied and dependent states. But it rested on conscription, taxation and the loyalty of reluctant allies.
Then Russia broke away from the Continental System, and both sides prepared for war.
On the twenty-fourth of June, 1812, the largest army Europe had ever seen began to cross the Niemen into Russia. Estimates for the whole invasion force range from about four hundred and fifty thousand to more than six hundred thousand men, and around half of them came from outside France.
The Russians retreated, refusing battle and burning supplies as they went. Heat, hunger, disease and desertion thinned the French ranks long before the first snow. Horses died by the thousands.
On the seventh of September, at Borodino, about a hundred kilometres west of Moscow, the armies finally met, in one of the bloodiest single days of the century. Tens of thousands fell on both sides, and the Russians withdrew in good order.
A week later, Napoleon entered Moscow. Within hours, fires broke out across the city, and most of Moscow burned. He waited more than a month for the Tsar to negotiate. No answer came.

[CH6 RETREAT, EXILE AND RETURN]
On the nineteenth of October, 1812, the Grande Armée left Moscow.
It was forced back along the same devastated road by which it had come. Hunger, disease, exhaustion and Cossack raids destroyed it piece by piece. Marshal Ney commanded the rearguard, fighting off attacks day after day, and the hard frosts of November finished what the march had begun.
At the river Berezina, in late November, engineers standing in icy water built the bridges that allowed part of the army to escape. In December, only a fraction of the force that had entered Russia struggled back across the Niemen. Napoleon had already left for Paris, to raise a new army.
He did raise one, and won more battles in the spring of 1813. But Prussia, and then Austria, joined Russia, Britain and Sweden against him. In October, at Leipzig, more than half a million soldiers fought for three days in the Battle of the Nations, and the coalition defeated him.
In 1814, allied armies invaded France. With a small force of veterans and young conscripts, Napoleon won a string of brilliant victories in February, but he could not be everywhere at once. Paris surrendered at the end of March, and his marshals refused to fight on. On the sixth of April, he abdicated.
The victors sent him into exile on Elba, a small island off the coast of Italy, and allowed him to keep the title of Emperor, of an island realm about thirty kilometres long.
He stayed less than a year.
On the first of March, 1815, Napoleon landed at Golfe-Juan, on the southern coast of France, with about a thousand men, and marched north through the mountains. Near Grenoble, a regiment sent to stop him lowered its muskets and joined him. Town after town opened its gates. On the twentieth of March, he was back in the Tuileries Palace in Paris. King Louis the Eighteenth had fled.

[CH7 WATERLOO, SAINT HELENA AND LEGACY]
The allies declared him an outlaw and mobilised more than half a million men. Napoleon decided to strike first, into Belgium, before their armies could unite.
On the sixteenth of June he beat the Prussians at Ligny, while Marshal Ney fought Wellington to a standstill at Quatre Bras. But the Prussians were beaten, not destroyed. They fell back to the north, toward Wavre, still within reach of Wellington.
Two days later came Waterloo.
In the evening, the Imperial Guard climbed the slope toward Wellington's line, through smoke and musket fire. Near the crest, the British and Dutch troops rose and fired at close range. For the first time, the Guard fell back. The cry went up across the field that the Guard was retreating, and with Prussian corps now pouring into its flank, the French army collapsed into flight.
In a single day, tens of thousands of men had been killed or wounded, in an area of a few square kilometres.
Four days later, Napoleon abdicated for the second time. He hoped to escape to America, but British warships blockaded the coast. In July, he surrendered to the captain of HMS Bellerophon.
This time the British sent him far away: to Saint Helena, a volcanic island in the South Atlantic, almost two thousand kilometres from the coast of Africa.
At Longwood House he dictated his memories to a few loyal companions, shaping the story of his life: the defender of the Revolution, the lawgiver, the soldier betrayed by fortune. Their books would build his legend.
He died there on the fifth of May, 1821, at the age of fifty-one, most probably of stomach cancer. In 1840, his remains were brought back to Paris, and laid to rest beneath the golden dome of Les Invalides.
What did he leave behind? The Civil Code, which shaped the law of countries across Europe and beyond. A centralised French state, with its prefects, its schools and its bank. A map of Germany and Italy that would never return to what it had been.
And the cost: wars that killed millions of soldiers and civilians, and, in 1802, the restoration of slavery in the French colonies, which the Revolution had abolished.
He rose through the Revolution, and ended it. He spread its laws on the points of bayonets. He built an empire that lasted barely a decade.
More than two hundred years later, Napoleon Bonaparte remains one of the most admired, and most argued-about, figures in history.
