The allies declared him an outlaw and mobilised more than half a million men. Napoleon decided to strike first, into Belgium, before their armies could unite.
On the sixteenth of June he beat the Prussians at Ligny, while Marshal Ney fought Wellington to a standstill at Quatre Bras. But the Prussians were beaten, not destroyed. They fell back to the north, toward Wavre, still within reach of Wellington.
Two days later came Waterloo.
In the evening, the Imperial Guard climbed the slope toward Wellington's line, through smoke and musket fire. Near the crest, the British and Dutch troops rose and fired at close range. For the first time, the Guard fell back. The cry went up across the field that the Guard was retreating, and with Prussian corps now pouring into its flank, the French army collapsed into flight.
In a single day, tens of thousands of men had been killed or wounded, in an area of a few square kilometres.
Four days later, Napoleon abdicated for the second time. He hoped to escape to America, but British warships blockaded the coast. In July, he surrendered to the captain of HMS Bellerophon.
This time the British sent him far away: to Saint Helena, a volcanic island in the South Atlantic, almost two thousand kilometres from the coast of Africa.
At Longwood House he dictated his memories to a few loyal companions, shaping the story of his life: the defender of the Revolution, the lawgiver, the soldier betrayed by fortune. Their books would build his legend.
He died there on the fifth of May, 1821, at the age of fifty-one, most probably of stomach cancer. In 1840, his remains were brought back to Paris, and laid to rest beneath the golden dome of Les Invalides.
What did he leave behind? The Civil Code, which shaped the law of countries across Europe and beyond. A centralised French state, with its prefects, its schools and its bank. A map of Germany and Italy that would never return to what it had been.
And the cost: wars that killed millions of soldiers and civilians, and, in 1802, the restoration of slavery in the French colonies, which the Revolution had abolished.
He rose through the Revolution, and ended it. He spread its laws on the points of bayonets. He built an empire that lasted barely a decade.
More than two hundred years later, Napoleon Bonaparte remains one of the most admired, and most argued-about, figures in history.
