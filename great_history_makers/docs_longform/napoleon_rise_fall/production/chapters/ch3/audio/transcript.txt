The Army of Italy was unpaid, underfed and badly equipped. Its new commander was twenty-six years old, and almost unknown.
Within weeks, he had split the armies of Piedmont and Austria, and forced Piedmont out of the war. Then he drove the Austrians across northern Italy, at Lodi, at Arcole and at Rivoli, and besieged their great fortress of Mantua until it surrendered.
He moved faster than his enemies thought possible, concentrated his forces where they were weakest, and wrote his own victory bulletins for the newspapers in Paris. At Arcole he was nearly killed leading an attack along a causeway through the marshes. A painting of that moment, a young general carrying a flag into the smoke, became one of the first images of his legend.
By 1797 he was dictating peace terms to Austria, without waiting for instructions from Paris.
The next year he sailed for Egypt, a bold plan to threaten Britain's route to India. With his soldiers came more than a hundred and fifty scholars and scientists, and during the expedition French soldiers uncovered the Rosetta Stone. On land he won battles, including the Battle of the Pyramids. But at Aboukir Bay, Admiral Nelson destroyed the French fleet, and the army was trapped. A campaign into Syria failed before the walls of Acre, and plague spread through the ranks.
In August 1799, Bonaparte left his army in Egypt and slipped back to France. He found the government divided and unpopular. In November he joined a coup that overthrew it, and became First Consul: in practice, the ruler of France.
Seven months later, he led an army across the Great Saint Bernard Pass and defeated the Austrians at Marengo. Peace with Austria, and then with Britain, followed. The general from Corsica was thirty years old, and France was his.
