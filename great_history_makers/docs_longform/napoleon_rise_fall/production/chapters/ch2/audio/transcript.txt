Napoleone di Buonaparte was born in Ajaccio, on the island of Corsica, on the fifteenth of August, 1769. Only a year earlier, the Republic of Genoa had handed the island to France, and French troops had just crushed the Corsican fight for independence.
His father, Carlo, was a lawyer from the minor nobility who made his peace with the new rulers. That status won his son a royal scholarship, and at the age of nine Napoleone was sent to the mainland: first to the military school at Brienne, then to the École Militaire in Paris.
He was an outsider, mocked for his accent and his poverty, and a voracious reader of history and mathematics. As a young man he dreamed of Corsican freedom, not of French glory. In 1785, at sixteen, he was commissioned as a second lieutenant of artillery, the most technical branch of the army, and one of the few open to a poor nobleman with talent.
Four years later, the French Revolution swept away the old monarchy. It also swept away much of the old officer corps, and opened careers to men of ability.
Bonaparte's chance came in 1793, at the port of Toulon, which royalists had handed over to a British fleet. The young artillery captain placed the guns that made the harbour impossible to hold. The British sailed away, and at twenty-four he was promoted to brigadier general.
The fall of Robespierre brought him a brief arrest and months without a command. Then, in October 1795, he helped the government crush a royalist uprising in the streets of Paris with artillery fire. The Republic now owed him a debt.
In March 1796 he married Joséphine de Beauharnais, a widow with powerful friends. Two days later he left Paris to take command of the Army of Italy.
