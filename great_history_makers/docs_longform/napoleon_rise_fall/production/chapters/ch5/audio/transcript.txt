In 1806, Prussia went to war, and its army was destroyed in a single day at Jena and Auerstedt. In 1807, after the frozen slaughter of Eylau and the victory of Friedland, Napoleon met Tsar Alexander on a raft in the middle of the river Niemen, and made peace at Tilsit.
He redrew the map of Europe. He abolished the thousand-year-old Holy Roman Empire, created the Confederation of the Rhine and the Duchy of Warsaw, and placed his brothers on the thrones of Holland, Naples, Westphalia and Spain.
To strangle Britain's economy, he ordered a blockade of British trade across the continent: the Continental System. But it was almost impossible to enforce. In Spain and Portugal it drew him into a long war of occupation, insurgency and British intervention, a war that would cost hundreds of thousands of lives.
In 1809 Austria rose again, and was defeated again, at Wagram. The next year Napoleon divorced Joséphine, who had given him no heir, and married the Austrian archduchess Marie-Louise. In 1811 their son was born, and given the title King of Rome.
On the map, the Empire was at its height. France itself stretched from Hamburg to Rome, surrounded by allied and dependent states. But it rested on conscription, taxation and the loyalty of reluctant allies.
Then Russia broke away from the Continental System, and both sides prepared for war.
On the twenty-fourth of June, 1812, the largest army Europe had ever seen began to cross the Niemen into Russia. Estimates for the whole invasion force range from about four hundred and fifty thousand to more than six hundred thousand men, and around half of them came from outside France.
The Russians retreated, refusing battle and burning supplies as they went. Heat, hunger, disease and desertion thinned the French ranks long before the first snow. Horses died by the thousands.
On the seventh of September, at Borodino, about a hundred kilometres west of Moscow, the armies finally met, in one of the bloodiest single days of the century. Tens of thousands fell on both sides, and the Russians withdrew in good order.
A week later, Napoleon entered Moscow. Within hours, fires broke out across the city, and most of Moscow burned. He waited more than a month for the Tsar to negotiate. No answer came.
