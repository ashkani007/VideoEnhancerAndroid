On the morning of the eighteenth of June, 1815, the fields south of Brussels were still soaked from a night of heavy rain.
Two armies faced each other across a shallow valley, roughly seventy thousand men on each side. On one ridge stood the Duke of Wellington, with an army of British, Dutch, Belgian and German troops. On the other stood Napoleon Bonaparte, Emperor of the French, back from exile for one last campaign.
He waited for the ground to dry before opening fire. The delay would matter. A Prussian army under Marshal Blücher, beaten by Napoleon only two days earlier, was marching toward the battlefield from the east. Wellington had chosen to fight here because Blücher had promised to come.
All afternoon, the French attacked. At the farm of Hougoumont, the fighting never stopped. At La Haye Sainte, the defenders held until their ammunition ran out. Wave after wave of French cavalry swept up the slope and broke around squares of redcoats that would not give way.
By early evening, Prussian troops were pushing into the village of Plancenoit, on the French right flank. Napoleon had one reserve left: the Imperial Guard, the veterans who had never been driven from a battlefield.
He ordered them forward.
What happened next would decide the fate of Europe. To understand how this man came to be standing on a muddy Belgian ridge, an emperor, an exile, and an emperor again, we have to go back almost half a century, to an island in the Mediterranean.
