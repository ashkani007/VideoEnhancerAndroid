On the nineteenth of October, 1812, the Grande Armée left Moscow.
It was forced back along the same devastated road by which it had come. Hunger, disease, exhaustion and Cossack raids destroyed it piece by piece. Marshal Ney commanded the rearguard, fighting off attacks day after day, and the hard frosts of November finished what the march had begun.
At the river Berezina, in late November, engineers standing in icy water built the bridges that allowed part of the army to escape. In December, only a fraction of the force that had entered Russia struggled back across the Niemen. Napoleon had already left for Paris, to raise a new army.
He did raise one, and won more battles in the spring of 1813. But Prussia, and then Austria, joined Russia, Britain and Sweden against him. In October, at Leipzig, more than half a million soldiers fought for three days in the Battle of the Nations, and the coalition defeated him.
In 1814, allied armies invaded France. With a small force of veterans and young conscripts, Napoleon won a string of brilliant victories in February, but he could not be everywhere at once. Paris surrendered at the end of March, and his marshals refused to fight on. On the sixth of April, he abdicated.
The victors sent him into exile on Elba, a small island off the coast of Italy, and allowed him to keep the title of Emperor, of an island realm about thirty kilometres long.
He stayed less than a year.
On the first of March, 1815, Napoleon landed at Golfe-Juan, on the southern coast of France, with about a thousand men, and marched north through the mountains. Near Grenoble, a regiment sent to stop him lowered its muskets and joined him. Town after town opened its gates. On the twentieth of March, he was back in the Tuileries Palace in Paris. King Louis the Eighteenth had fled.
